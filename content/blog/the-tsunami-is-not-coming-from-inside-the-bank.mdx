# The Tsunami Is Not Coming from Inside the Bank

A couple in Winterthur wants to buy a house. They earn well, have saved diligently, and qualify comfortably. Ten years ago, they would have walked into their Hausbank and started the conversation there. Five years ago, they might have compared three offers. Today, they ask their AI agent to find the best mortgage in Switzerland.

The agent does not walk into a bank. **It contacts thirty of them. Simultaneously.** Each request is perfectly formatted. Each carries the same financial profile. The couple's effort is unchanged. They are still buying one house.

The banks are now processing ten times more applications for the same number of houses.

## One house, thirty applications

Most of the conversation about agentic AI in banking focuses on what happens inside the institution — how agents will process loans, handle compliance, manage onboarding. That is the supply side. It matters.

**But the pressure arrives from outside first.** From customers, or more precisely, from the AI agents acting on their behalf.

The behaviour is obvious once you see it. A competent agent optimising for its principal will query every viable lender, not just the three the client would have contacted manually. If the average customer moves from 3 banks to 30, every bank sees roughly **ten times more inbound volume**. The mortgage market has not grown. The application market has exploded.

## The arithmetic of ruin

Here is where the model breaks. Follow the numbers for a single Swiss bank.

**Today:** Based on industry estimates, processing one mortgage application costs roughly CHF 1'500. The win rate is about one in three. Revenue per mortgage is around CHF 20'000. Expected value per application: CHF 6'700. Comfortable.

**In the agentic world:** The win rate drops to one in thirty. The cost per application has not changed. Expected value per application: CHF 667.

**Every application you process but do not win now costs you CHF 833.**

To break even, processing cost must fall from CHF 1'500 to under CHF 667. A 56 percent reduction. Not through better spreadsheets or leaner teams. Through a structurally different process.

Most banks cannot halve their cost by optimising what they already do. **The economics do not demand efficiency. They demand automation.**

## The three-day bank and the three-hour bank

There is a second dynamic, and it is less forgiving.

Picture the couple in Winterthur again. Their agent has sent thirty requests. Within two hours, eight banks have returned indicative offers. By evening, three have provided binding terms. The couple's agent ranks them, highlights the trade-offs, and recommends a shortlist.

Bank number twenty-three responds on Wednesday. Its offer is competitive. **It is also irrelevant.** The couple signed on Monday.

This is the cruelty of the new model. The slow bank did the work. It ran the credit check. It prepared the offer. It spent the CHF 1'500. And it lost — not because its product was worse, but because **it arrived after the decision was made**.

Today, response time is a service metric. In an agentic market, it is the conversion driver. The bank that responds while the customer is still engaged wins disproportionately. The bank that responds after has performed free labour.

## Free price discovery

This leads to the most uncomfortable implication.

In the old model, every bank that submitted an offer had a reasonable chance of winning. The customer compared three, maybe four. The effort of applying created a natural floor on conversion rates.

In the agentic model, **slow banks become free price discovery tools**. The customer's agent collects their offers not to buy from them, but to benchmark against the offers it has already received. The bank provides data. Someone else provides the mortgage.

The work is identical. The cost is identical. **The revenue is zero.**

## Beyond mortgages

The mortgage example is vivid, but the pattern is not unique to mortgages. It applies to any product where a customer's agent can query multiple banks simultaneously.

Corporate lending. Foreign exchange. Trade finance. Insurance. **Any product where comparison was previously limited by the customer's time and effort** will see the same dynamics: volume explosion, win rate compression, speed premium, and the inversion of who captures value.

The [agentic target operating model](/blog/agentic-tom-introduction) is a **structural response to a structural change in how demand reaches the bank**. Customers are not changing what they want. They are changing how they ask for it.

## The moat and the deadline

The institutions that automate first will not just survive the volume. They will use it.

Ten times more inbound means ten times more data. Ten times more pricing signal. Ten times more opportunity to learn what customers actually want and what they will actually accept. For the banks that can process at low marginal cost and respond in hours — with [escalation designed in](/blog/escalation-by-design), not bolted on — **the tsunami is not a threat. It is a moat.**

The couple in Winterthur does not care about any of this. They want a good rate, a fast answer, and a bank they trust. Their agent will find all three.

**The question is whether your bank is one of them.** Or whether it is the one that spent CHF 1'500 preparing an offer nobody read.
