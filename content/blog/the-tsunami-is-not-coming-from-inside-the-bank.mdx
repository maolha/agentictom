# The Tsunami Is Not Coming from Inside the Bank

Most of the conversation about agentic AI in banking focuses on what happens inside the institution. How agents will process loans, manage compliance, handle onboarding. That is the supply side. It matters. **But it is not where the pressure arrives first.**

The pressure arrives from outside. From customers — or more precisely, **from the AI agents acting on their behalf**.

## The mortgage example

Consider the Swiss mortgage market. Today, a customer looking for a mortgage contacts three to five banks. They compare offers manually. The process is slow, friction-heavy, and favours the bank with the existing relationship.

Now give that customer an AI agent.

The agent does not contact three banks. **It contacts thirty.** Requests dispatched in parallel, each perfectly formatted, each carrying the same financial profile. The customer's effort is unchanged. The bank's inbound volume is not.

This is not speculative. It is the obvious behaviour of any competent agent optimising for its principal. If the average customer moves from querying 3 banks to querying 30, every bank in the market sees its inbound application volume increase roughly tenfold. **The number of houses being bought has not changed. The number of applications has.**

## The unit economics collapse

This is where the model breaks.

Today, a Swiss bank processes a mortgage application at a cost of roughly CHF 1,500. The win rate is approximately one in three. At CHF 20,000 revenue per mortgage, the expected value per application is around CHF 6,700. **The unit economics are comfortable.**

In an agentic world, the win rate drops to one in thirty. The cost per application has not changed. The expected value per application falls to CHF 667. **Every application you process but do not win costs you CHF 833.**

To break even at the new win rate, processing cost per application must fall from CHF 1,500 to under CHF 667. That is a **56 percent cost reduction** — not through incremental efficiency, but through a fundamentally different way of handling volume.

Most banks cannot halve their processing cost by optimising the existing model. **The economics demand automation.**

## Speed becomes the differentiator

There is a second dynamic. When thirty banks receive the same request simultaneously, **the ones that respond within hours have a structural advantage** over the ones that take days. The customer's agent is not waiting. It is collecting, comparing, and ranking in real time.

A bank that returns a credible offer within a few hours is in the consideration set. **A bank that takes three days is providing a data point for someone else's comparison.** The effort is identical. The outcome is not.

Today, response time is a service quality metric. In an agentic market, **it is a conversion driver**. The bank that responds while the customer is still engaged wins disproportionately. The bank that responds after the decision is made has done free work.

A process built around manual review, committee approval, and email-based communication cannot consistently respond in hours at ten times the current volume. **An automated process can.**

## The inversion

Here is the part that should concern every head of mortgage lending in Switzerland: in this model, slow banks still process the applications. They spend the CHF 1,500. They run the credit checks. They prepare the offer. And then they lose. Consistently.

**They become free price discovery tools for AI agents.** They do the work. Someone else collects the revenue.

## What this means for the operating model

The mortgage example is illustrative, but **the pattern generalises**. Any product where customers can use AI agents to query multiple providers simultaneously will exhibit the same dynamics: volume increase, win rate compression, speed premium, and the inversion of who captures value.

This is why the agentic TOM is not a technology initiative. It is a **structural response to a structural change in how demand reaches the bank**. The customers are not changing what they want. They are changing how they ask for it. And that change alone is enough to break the model most Swiss banks currently operate.

The institutions that automate first will not just survive the volume. They will use it. Ten times more inbound means ten times more data, ten times more pricing signal, ten times more opportunity to learn. **For the banks that can process at low marginal cost and respond in hours, the tsunami is not a threat. It is a moat.**

For everyone else, it is a deadline.
