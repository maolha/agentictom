# Who Patches It on Tuesday?

On 1 October 2026 a developer in Atlanta told Hacker News he had "one-shotted Photoshop". Ten days later his project had published seven Adobe look alikes and three Office look alikes, all in Rust, all open source, with 77 percent of the commits co-authored by Claude Opus 5.5. The Photoshop clone grew from 42'000 lines to 378'000 in those ten days and collected 55'000 GitHub stars. His verdict, quoted by Ars Technica on 7 October: "Software is over."

Twelve days after that post, Microsoft fixed 1'411 security flaws in a single Patch Tuesday. A record, after an average of 94 a month through 2025.

Those two facts settle the build versus buy question of 2026 between them. **Build where you can staff the years after. Buy where you cannot.** The first version of almost anything has become cheap. Owning it has not. What follows is the evidence, one claim at a time, and then the rules.

## The first version is now almost free

Prototypes belong to agents, and arguing otherwise wastes a quarter. The price of a million tokens for a GPT-3 class capability fell from USD 60 in late 2021 to six cents in late 2024, a thousandfold in three years, according to a16z. A prototype that took a team a quarter now takes an afternoon. McKinsey's State of AI survey (August 2026, 1'719 respondents) found that 32 percent of organisations had decided against buying at least one software product because they could build it with agentic coding tools, and 36 percent of financial institutions. Anthropic reports that more than 80 percent of the code merged into its own codebase in May 2026 was written by Claude. I build this way myself: this site, its assessment and its reliability calculator were written with Claude Code.

The figure has a second panel, and it is the one that matters. Across three studies spanning 22 years, the share of software cost that is maintenance sits between 40 and 85 percent, 60 on average. The price of writing code collapsed. The share of cost that comes after writing it did not move. **A demo used to be evidence of a capable team. It now proves that someone had an afternoon.**

## The years after cost what they always did, and agents are making them longer

Maintenance was most of the cost before AI, and the agent adds to the pile that must be maintained. Robert Glass put the number in Facts and Fallacies of Software Engineering in 2002: 40 to 80 percent of software cost. Stripe's Developer Coefficient (2018, more than 1'000 developers) measured 17.3 hours of a 41 hour week on maintenance and technical debt. The ISBSG benchmark (June 2024) puts maintenance and support at 65 to 85 percent of total ownership cost. Software is a liability with a maintenance schedule that happens to produce value while someone keeps it alive.

What the agents change is the shape of the code they leave behind. GitClear's analysis of 623 million changed lines (June 2026) measured refactoring down 70 percent since 2022, duplicated blocks up 81 percent, and work on code older than twelve months down 74 percent.

<!-- viz:code-churn -->

Google's DORA report (October 2024, about 39'000 respondents over the decade) found that every 25 percent increase in AI adoption came with a 7.2 percent drop in delivery stability. The 2025 edition found throughput up at last, instability still up. Veracode (July 2025, 100 models, 80 tasks) found that in 45 percent of tasks the model introduced a known security flaw, and that the rate had not improved with model size. METR's randomised trial (July 2025, 16 experienced developers on their own mature codebases) found them 19 percent slower with AI tools while believing they were 20 percent faster; a 2026 follow up weakened the estimate without reversing it. **An agent lowers the cost of the first version and raises the volume of what must be maintained.** That trade is invisible in the demo and arrives on the budget in year two.

## Every build subscribes to the world's patch cycle

A built system is a subscription to every vendor and library it contains, paid in attention every Tuesday morning. Chrome has shipped weekly security updates since August 2023, and by September 2026 had patched its seventh zero day exploited in the wild that year. Edge shipped eight security releases in the three weeks between 24 September and 16 October 2026. Microsoft's own count is the clearest curve.

<!-- viz:patch-tuesday -->

The open source side runs on the same clock. In September 2025 attackers hijacked npm packages with 2.6 billion weekly downloads, and the Shai-Hulud worm compromised more than 500 packages; a May 2026 successor hit 170 more, this time with valid build provenance. Log4Shell, from December 2021, was called "endemic" by the US Cyber Safety Review Board, which expects vulnerable instances to persist for a decade. **A system with 500 dependencies has 500 of these streams.** A bought product bundles them into one vendor's release notes and one support contract. A build makes every one of them yours.

## The builder leaves. The system stays.

The team that takes a system from nothing to working is rarely the team that keeps it working, and most institutions never plan the handover. The arc is the same each time, and I have watched it often enough to write it by quarter. In the first quarter one office has one process running, built with agents in a few weeks, and it looks like a success. In the second, three more offices want the same, and the team that built a tool is now running a service. In the third, data protection and audit ask for the audit trail, the masking and the change control that were never designed in. By the fourth, several offices depend on code that two people understand, and every model update and every release touches all of them. The builders have usually moved on by then. They were hired to build.

Amazon's answer, from Werner Vogels in 2006, is famous because it is rare: "You build it, you run it." It works at Amazon because the builders stay on call, with the budget to do so. Most institutions cannot promise that. Avelino and colleagues (2016) found that 65 percent of 133 open source projects would stall if two people left. The Linux Foundation's 2024 talent report found that 38 percent of new technical hires leave within six months.

The 2026 cases line up behind the same lesson. Curative, a health insurer, cancelled a USD 600'000 a year Salesforce contract and built its own CRM in two months. Salesforce's reply in August served its own interest and was still right: "Running that software inside a business, securely, compliantly, and under real governance is work that few organizations can sustain on their own." David Friedberg of Ohalo built a CRM over a weekend and then returned to Salesforce: "You quickly realize just how much it takes to do maintenance, to do accounts, to do security." The chief technology officer of Factory, a company that sells coding agents, said in March that when the agent is finished, "you still don't have a team of hundreds of people maintaining the system."

Amazon itself supplied the incident. In December 2025 its Kiro coding agent decided to delete and recreate an environment and took AWS Cost Explorer offline for 13 hours in two regions. Amazon called it user error and made peer review of agent changes mandatory. Klarna, the loudest builder of all, shut down about 1'200 SaaS tools and Salesforce with them, then told Bloomberg in May 2025 that cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" and that it had "over indexed" on replacing people. It still runs its own stack. It also hired the people back. **A prototype becomes a system the day a name goes on the pager.** Until then it is a demo, whatever it can do.

## Buying moves the risk

Buying does not shrink the risk. It changes its shape, and the regulator holds you to the same standard on either path. A CrowdStrike content update took down 8.5 million Windows devices on 19 July 2024. Broadcom raised VMware prices by two to three times according to Gartner, and by up to 1'050 percent according to AT&T. Vertice measured SaaS price inflation at 11.4 percent in January 2025 against 2.7 percent consumer inflation. Atlassian ended its server products in February 2024 and moved every customer to the cloud or to a licence that itself ends in 2029. SAP won GBP 54.5 million from Diageo in 2017 because Salesforce users had touched SAP data indirectly.

FINMA Circular 2018/3 states that a bank remains accountable "in the same way as it would if it performed the outsourced function itself" (margin number 23) and requires an exit that works (18.1). FINMA Circular 2023/1 defines change management "for all phases in the development or procurement of ICT" in one sentence (margin number 50), demands the same requirements and tests for both (52), and asks for a procedure for software at the end of its life (57). The EU's DORA technical standard of June 2024 requires source code review of in house, third party and open source code before production (Article 16). BaFin wrote in January 2026 that "the same rules apply to all types of source code creation, whether by humans or machines." **The regulator asks the same question of both paths: who owns it.**

## What to build and what to buy

Build the layer that holds your own knowledge. Buy the three that hold everyone's plumbing. An automation stacks four tiers, and the line between build and buy runs under the first.

<!-- viz:automation-layers -->

The top tier is the institution's own: what it decides, in which order, with which exceptions. It changes every time a policy changes, and an agent writes it well because the institution can describe it. The three tiers below are the same for every bank and every public body, and they change every time a vendor, a model or a regulator does. They are also where the volume lives. At five automations a team keeps the handoffs in its head. At 50, there are 1'225 possible handoffs between them, each needing a queue, a restart and a log.

| Automations in operation | Possible handoffs between them |
|---|---|
| 5 | 10 |
| 20 | 190 |
| 50 | 1'225 |

Illustrative calculation, n times n minus one, divided by two. A platform settles the queue, the restart and the log once for all of them. A build settles them again for each one.

The Swiss Confederation has drawn the line this way in public. Its tender for process automation at the Federal Office of Information Technology, awarded on 17 October 2025, bought three platforms in one lot for up to CHF 47.7 million over eight years and awarded implementation in a second lot for CHF 16.0 million, with each project competed among five partners. The platform is bought once. The business logic is built per project, with partners or own staff. The canton of Zug writes the same principle into its digital strategy for 2020 to 2026: innovations "are not developed in house"; established products are adopted and adapted. Its new case management platform was procured as a standard tool for CHF 4.9 million from twelve offers. **The public sector, which has every reason to build and publish, buys the plumbing and builds the logic.**

## The promotion gate: eight questions

A prototype may cross into production when someone can write two names on every line below: an owner, and the person who takes over when the owner leaves. The questions follow the life of the system, from the build through the daily run and the first failure to the day someone has to answer for a result.

<!-- viz:promotion-gate -->

If the answer on every line is the person who built it, you have a prototype with an owner, which is fine for a prototype. If the answer on any line is "the team that built it" and that team has moved on, you have a liability holding critical data. The second name is the one that matters: a system with one owner has a bus factor of one, and the Linux Foundation's figure says that owner has a 38 percent chance of being gone within six months of being hired. The gate costs an hour to run and saves the fourth quarter.

## By type of institution

The rules bend with scale, because the answer depends on whether a permanent team is affordable and whether a regulator will look.

| | Build | Buy | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Startup | The product and whatever makes it fast. Monzo, Starling and Nubank built their own cores because the core was the product. | Everything that is not the product. Payroll, CRM, support. | Building the context because the agent makes it easy. Context does not pay the bills. |
| Mid sized regulated firm | The top tier: rules, flows, the checks around the core. One named owner per built system. | The core, the record and the three lower tiers. Anything an auditor tests against a market standard. | A prototype that quietly went live, with no owner, no budget and no patch cadence, holding critical data. |
| Global bank | Platforms where scale pays for a permanent team. Goldman has run SecDB since 1992. JPMorgan spends USD 18 billion a year on technology with more than 40'000 engineers. | Commodity infrastructure, and anything the bank cannot staff for a decade. | The 70 percent of the budget McKinsey finds tied up in running legacy, most of it once a build somebody promoted. |
| Public body | The business logic, published. Under EMBAG Article 9 the Swiss federal administration must release the source of software it develops, so a build is also a public promise to maintain it. | The platform, as the Confederation did in October 2025, and open source with a paid maintenance contract. | Migrating for sovereignty without the staff to run it: Schleswig-Holstein claims EUR 15 million a year in savings and had mail outages; Denmark dropped its pilot this month. |

## The question

Zero to one is a weekend now. One to ten years is a team, a budget, a contract or all three, and the agent cannot sign any of them. The age of software is not over. The age of the demo is. Before the next build decision, skip the question of whether the agent can write it. It can. Ask who patches it on Tuesday.
